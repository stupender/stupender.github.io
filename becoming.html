No. 02 Case study · 2026 – present

Becoming

A local-first companion that shows whether your time is going where your values are — vision, projects and the day's hours on one ladder.

For
Someone with more intentions than hours, at the start of a day
Role
Product design & build, solo — with AI doing the typing
Timeline
A spreadsheet, Aug 2026 · an app since Sep · in daily use
Stack
TypeScript · React · Express · SQLite · Electron · sql.js
Links
No live link, no public source — the data is the point

In short

The problem
I couldn't tell where my time actually went. As I put it to a guitar student a month before I designed any of this: “I'm not a reliable accountant of my time and experience.”
My role
Sole designer and builder. AI wrote most of the code; I decided the structure, and rewrote the words until they said something exact.

The outcome
Open on 58 of the last 60 days. A second person has used it on her own iPad since October. A guitar student has asked for a copy — and wants the one feature the app refuses to build.

01 · The problem

I was an unreliable witness to my own time

I'm a freelance sound artist and teacher. Nothing told me where a week went. I could finish a week certain a project was failing and be wrong, because the judgment came from my mood rather than from anything I could look at. I said this out loud in a guitar lesson in September, before any of it was designed:

“Sometimes I feel like I'm a bad… I'm not a reliable accountant of my time and experience. Depending on my mood and a million other factors, I could be like, this project sucks, and it's failing.”

— me, in a lesson, 29 September 2026

My student answered “I know exactly what you mean.” That exchange is the brief. Not a tracker — something that makes a week legible enough that I stop guessing at it. It began as an income and expense spreadsheet, to check whether my spending matched what I said I valued; the question turned out not to be about money. Money is only the part that arrives with numbers attached.

The tools meant to help were the opposite of help. Open a budgeting app and something is red. Open a habit tracker and a streak is about to break. Scoring a life produces the state that makes good decisions impossible. A ledger you avoid is worse than no ledger.

Money also turned out to be only the loudest place energy goes. The body, attention, relationships and creative work are others, and they lived in five different apps that never spoke. So Becoming holds them together under one question: is where my energy actually goes matching what I say matters?

What made it hard is that the honest answer is often “not this month.” The design problem was never “visualise personal data.” It was build an honest instrument that a person will still open on a bad month.

02 · Constraints

Private data, one person, and someone who isn't me

One person, no budget. I'm the designer, the developer and, at first, the only user. No paid Apple developer account, no hosting bill, no team — every feature had to be worth maintaining alone.

The data is the most private there is. Bank statements, bloodwork, how I felt this morning. Local-first isn't a feature here; it's the premise. Nothing goes to a server I don't own — and as it turned out, nothing goes to a server at all.

It has to work for someone who isn't me. Partway through, the brief widened: my partner wanted a copy, starting from empty. That meant no personal content in the shipped code, a first run that works on a blank database, and an install that needs no technical help.

So the server runs inside the page. I first did the obvious thing — left the Mac on and reached it from the iPad over a private tunnel, with a passphrase door in front. It worked and it was wrong: a computer running at home, a network to keep healthy, a door that has to be right every time. So I sent the server to the iPad instead. The same server code is bundled into the web app, with SQLite compiled to WebAssembly and the database in the browser's own storage. Requests to /api never leave the device; they're answered in-process. There is no server to leak from, because there is no server.

And one rule, kept in the code. Derived reads return counts, shares and ranges — never scores. An area holding nothing is not failing; it may simply be a stretch nobody has read yet. The rule lives where data is produced, so no screen can render a grade by accident. It's a guardrail, not the point. The point is seeing where the time went.

“It reports; it does not grade. An area with nothing in it is not failing.”

— the comment above the code that computes it

Two smaller rules come from the same place. The app never writes the person's words — vision statements, intentions and feeling notes are only ever theirs. And any interpretive text restates a source and cites it: the astrology readings quote the book and page they come from rather than inventing a softer line. The mirror can be wrong, but it can't make things up.

03 · The decision

One page at three sizes, so the day can point upward

The app could show where my hours went. It could not show what they were for. Vision lived on one page, projects on another, the day on a third, and nothing joined them except my memory — which is the faculty that started this whole problem.

So vision, project and action became three sizes of the same page. Each sits inside exactly one thing above it, which makes the way back a single line: the trail at the top of every page — Work › A studio that sustains itself › Song Catalog. An hour now has an address. The time it took reaches the vision it serves without my having to remember the chain, which is the entire question the app exists to answer.

What I gave up was a clean taxonomy. A project is finite: stages, dates, a price. A collection never finishes — a song catalog, a set of students. I expected that line to be obvious and in use it isn't. So the kind is a switch, with “nothing is lost — switch back any time” printed beside it. Naming the ambiguity in the interface was cheaper and more honest than pretending I could resolve it for everybody.

One rule underneath keeps it from lying. An hour can serve more than one page, but it is counted in exactly one. The pages that gather an hour and the figures that count it read from different places, so an hour of study that also serves a second project is still one hour, in one column. A tool whose purpose is an honest account cannot double-count the thing it accounts for.

04 · The insight

Rewriting the labels was how I found the design

AI wrote most of the code here, and it writes fluent, vague copy. A field that chose which project a task served was labelled “Serves.” It reads like meaning and decides nothing. Rewriting it was the design work, not tidying up after it. “Serves” became Project — named for the page it selects from, because that is what the person is actually choosing. “To-do” became Action. Every time a label came out vague, something underneath it was undecided.

One sentence I wrote while trying to say what two lists were for became an entire feature:

“What's Happening Today is just unscheduled, and the Shape of the Day is scheduled.”

— my own note, 29 September 2026, now quoted in the code it produced

Before it, the day was one undifferentiated list. After it, the day has two halves that behave differently — hours you committed to, and work not yet placed in time — and the distinction spread into practices, projects and the archive. I read all 1,190 strings in the app in one pass and rewrote 28 of them. The copy audit was a design audit wearing other clothes.

The same discipline caught a lie in the money model. It kept telling me I was falling short. I don't type expenses in as they happen; I bring in statements on a couple of fixed days a month, so the current month always looked thin and the floor always looked missed. I wrote:

“I don't input my expenses until some day in the future, the money date. So this will always look like I'm not hitting the mark.”

— my own note, from using it on real months

That changed the model, not just the chart. Every money reading now asks one question first: is this stretch read in all the way, part of the way, or not at all? Only a stretch read in all the way is set against the floor or coloured as covered or short. The headline is the last whole month; the month still open is drawn as a dashed outline that says when it comes in, never as a bar that came up short. The week's and the day's pages say the same thing in words: this week's money comes in with the money date, Oct 15.

The same lesson — design for how the person actually works, not how the data would like them to — moved the day around too. Unscheduled to-dos used to sit at the top. I asked for them to go below the hours, “so I'm not consumed so much by the unfinished, unscheduled things, and that my focus is first on what is on my calendar.” Weekly practices got their own days for the same reason: they appear on the days they belong to, and on other days only as a quiet line — still open this week — instead of a to-do that nags all seven.

The design system

One control grammar, read as paper

Growing fast across thirty screens, the app accumulated the ordinary disease: every page had invented its own way to add a row, remove one, or mark something done. The fix was a single vocabulary every surface builds from. Dot for a thing you tick — hollow is still yours to call, filled is done. Mark for a state that simply was — a square, because a circle reads as something you can press. Row for a ruled entry, never a card. Inputs are bare, sitting on a rule — a boxed field reads as a form to fill in, and this needed to read as a notebook. Native browser dialogs are banned outright, because a system alert breaks the page's voice mid-sentence.

The feelings grid is that grammar under the most pressure. It's built on the Ekmans' Atlas of Emotions — five families, each holding states of different intensity, plus a row for the ordinary words outside them. It's multi-select, because a day is rarely one feeling, and tapping is the whole interaction. Saying what a feeling was about is a second, optional gesture, because a feeling alone is weather and a feeling with its object is material. Nothing is counted: no streak, no “days without anger.”

Night mode is its own palette, not an inversion: a near-black taken from this portfolio's charcoal, quieter rules, no paper grain, and the day's terracotta lifted just enough to hold against the dark.

What didn't work

The app lost my own writing

In October I dragged an image into a note and everything below it disappeared. The editor had no handling for a file drop, so it replaced the selection with a node it couldn't build, and took the text under the caret with it.

The fix is two-part: the editor now refuses pictures outright rather than half-accepting them, and every prose field keeps its last twenty versions before any save. A save that truncates can no longer be the only copy.

The tail of that evening's notes was not recoverable. I went through the write-ahead log, the freed pages, 54 backups and the journal files, and it was gone. Local-first means nobody else has a copy either — the premise working exactly as designed, and it cuts both ways. I had written that guarantee down many times without once reading it as a risk.

Two more, in the same promise. The server bound every network interface by default, which meant that for a while anyone on the same wi-fi could have read the whole database just by knowing the address. Nothing in the interface hinted at it. It now listens to the machine it's on and nothing else — and the iPad version has no network surface at all.

Smaller things failed and came out as fast. A calendar moved into the side menu lasted an afternoon (“no, I don't like it, put the calendar back”). A new palette for time blocks was, in my own review, horrendous — so the colours went back to warm-to-cool across the day, with the new spectrum offered beside them as a choice rather than a replacement.

Onboarding lost the first thing someone wrote. Code written against my own full database assumed areas and settings existed. On an empty one it failed silently and dropped a new user's first vision statement, at exactly the moment trust is decided. First run is now tested on a blank database as its own case, because “works on my machine” and “works on an empty one” are different claims.

05 · Outcome

Open on 58 of the last 60 days — and one request I can't grant

Becoming runs as a packaged Mac app and as a Home Screen web app that holds everything on the device. It's 30 screens, 113 components and 74 server modules — about 63,000 lines of TypeScript across 293 commits since September — on one control grammar, with the architecture and the design system written down and kept current. It reads bank and brokerage statements locally, deduplicates every row so re-importing never double-counts, and reconciles to each statement's own totals to the penny.

I open it on 58 of the last 60 days. My partner has her own copy on her own iPad, holding her data and nobody else's. Her use is the best design input I've had, and almost none of it is praise. The onboarding questions were vague about where an answer would land, so each one now says — the last one saves a first vision, word for word. She doesn't open the money section at all, which tells me something is in the way there that I can't see, because I built it.

And a guitar student has asked for a copy, because the question is the one we already work on in lessons. He wants something specific:

“I know the stuff that I'm working on in my head, but it would be nice to be able to, like, visualize… almost see, like, a progress bar.”

— a guitar student, 29 September 2026

A progress bar is the one thing the app refuses to build. My first outside request pulls directly against my central rule, and I don't think he's wrong. That is unresolved, and it is the most useful thing anyone has told me about it.

The honest framing: two people use this, not two thousand. No public source, because local-first is the premise. I won't claim adoption it doesn't have. What it shows is a product held to one constraint long enough that the constraint shaped the data layer, the API, the components and the copy.

06 · Reflection

I built it for one person, and that was the limit

I'd design for more than myself from the start. I made a tool for me. Watching someone else use it showed me how much of it could serve other people, and that I hadn't thought big enough about what it could be. The student asking for a copy made that plain.

The planning can become the avoidance. My partner spends a long time adding pictures to her projects and visions, making the pages beautiful. I recognise it, because I do it too: the arranging feels like the work and isn't. The app currently rewards it. I don't yet know how to design against that without turning the thing into a nag, which would break the one rule I care about. That's the open problem.

Say what things are. The most useful tool I had was refusing vague words. When a label wouldn't come out plain, the design underneath it wasn't finished. With an AI producing fluent copy on demand, that stopped being a style preference and became the main way I found the decisions.

I'd have used it on real days sooner. The money-date insight was invisible on test data and obvious within a month of my own. And put the principle where it can be enforced — “don't grade the person” survived contact with real features because the API can't emit a grade. A principle in a doc is a preference; a principle in a contract is a constraint.

And the one that generalises furthest: a product's promise has an architecture, or it isn't a promise. “Your data stays with you” was a sentence on a settings page until the network said otherwise. Design that stops at the surface can't tell the difference — which is a large part of why I build the things I design.
