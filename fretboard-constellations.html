Overview

A theory tool built by the teacher who needed it

Fretboard Constellations shows music theory on the guitar neck. Each scale degree has its own colour. Scales, chords and inversions appear in every position, with notation and TAB, and all of it plays. Behind a switch sits a lead-sheet workbench that names what every chord in a progression is doing.

I've taught music for fifteen years. I designed the screens and ruled on the fingerings; Claude Code wrote the TypeScript. It's my first app built with Claude Code to ship: 226 commits between 15 June and 19 October 2026.

The thread through my work is a tool for someone in the middle of something, built to get out of their way. Here that someone is me, mid-lesson. Scroll to the music and the controls fold to one line, while the neck stays pinned.

01 · The problem

Theory books hold still. A lesson doesn't.

A lesson moves. A student brings a song in an awkward key, or a chord we haven't covered, and the material has to follow. Books and handouts are drawn in one key, so I would stop teaching to transpose.

I said it plainly to JA in August: “In the past… you have to write out, like, 12 sets of these for every key.” In July, mid-lesson, I lost my place working out a chord pair: “This is why we need the app. Chord pair, give me all the inversions.”

I'd had the idea since 2016, along with an ear-training quiz. In 2020 I started designing the visuals in Figma, page by page, as handouts. A handout still has to be redrawn for every key.

02 · Constraints

Thirty days of commits, a token limit, and code a small model could maintain

I had a light coding background and design skills, but never the time for a project this size. Claude Code made fast prototyping possible, and I iterated with it from there — “pretty much running through my tokens always.”

It took 11 weeks from first commit to v1, with commits on 24 of those days and almost none from mid-July to late August. The brief on day one called me a “novice coder” and set a rule: the code “must be maintainable later by a small local AI model.” Boring, conventional, heavily commented, small files.

That rule shaped the architecture. Theory is data, not code: a scale, chord, tuning or instrument is a data file the engine reads. There's no backend; it's a static site.

The ear-training quiz — the other half of the 2016 idea — reads the same data. It only asks about chords that can occur in the keys and scales you've put in play. Honestly, I've barely used it in lessons yet.

03 · The insight

The name came out of a lesson, six days before the code

On 9 June I was transcribing Quincy Jones with SF. I said a player almost has to “see the fretboard lit up as like a little constellation” of the notes in the key. Two candidate keys might show as “your red color and… your blue color.”

SF answered: “I haven't developed that relationship with the fretboard yet…” That's the person the app is for, saying what's missing. The first commit was six days later.

The brief's first line was “a living textbook.” I wanted the screen to do what a sheet of paper used to, then hand the guitar back.

04 · The decision

Light the whole key, not one box

Guitarists usually learn scales as box shapes, one hand position at a time. The app offers those — CAGED, and three, four or five notes per string. But it opens on every note of the key, everywhere on the neck, and the boxes are a scroll away. I gave up the familiar starting point to get the whole map.

The evidence came on 30 August. JJ-B improvised in E♭ with every note lit: “It feels good. Just being able to just take a look here and… see what different intervals sound like together.” I heard him going for far more variety.

Twenty minutes later he described the change himself. He'd started “just going through the scale.” Now he was “starting to hear… what you're playing,” and looking for “something that kind of meshes with that.” By October he was explaining target notes back to me.

“I don't think I've ever seen, like, a chart with, like, you know, the colors, and then, like… the intervals right there.”

— JJ-B, guitar student, comparing it to guitar books

05 · What didn't work

Too much at once, and not yet self-explanatory

Showing a chord on every string set at once overwhelmed RV: “The plot last week was overwhelming, it was like, I bit off more than I could actually…” I cut it to one string set a week, and sent him that one box as a screenshot.

The screen still needs me to explain it. SF had to ask “How do you see the degree?” — although the colour key was already on the neck. Later he asked “What do the lines in the fretboard mean?” From the first demo, the open question was the flow: why you'd reach for it, and when. It still is.

The data rule leaked, too. The brief said never hard-code six strings. When ukulele arrived on 1 September, two hidden six-string assumptions broke it: TAB height, and scale boxes that asked for two octaves. Both were fixed that day. The theory layer took the new instrument as pure data.

06 · Outcome

Four students, one breakthrough on record, no numbers yet

It shipped on 1 September as an installable web app. Since late August I've used it in lessons with four students. SF was first: he saw it the day after the first commit, asked me to send it, and offered to be the beta tester. His heat-map idea went into the backlog three days later and shipped in two weeks.

JA worked through inversions on screen. On 24 September he realised the C chord he plays most is an inversion. By 15 October: “I'm gonna actually do some more diving in it myself.” RV asks for screenshots, so he can print them.

What isn't known is how many people use it. There's no analytics. An email sign-up shipped, and I've sent it to a few guitar communities. I won't claim adoption it doesn't have.

07 · Reflection

The next feature is a lock

My students mostly got screenshots and PDFs, not the app. That was partly my hesitation to share it before it was ready, and before I was committed to it. I wouldn't change that.

What I'd add is a focused exercise for one student, locked to one setting, so they practise instead of clicking around. Half of it exists: a link that opens the app in an exact setting. The lock doesn't.

This is how I'd work with AI on a team. The model writes the code; I bring the rules it can't know, and check them against the instrument. One correction is in the log verbatim: “It is clearly not that fingering for the 5 Shapes on E phrygian.” I'm not always right either. I once read a line-drawing bug as wrong notes, and a check of 71,540 shapes proved me wrong.
